### 2. The Weight of the Real: A Phenomenological Exploration of the "Excess of Reality" in Borderline Consciousness Through a Comparison with Schizophrenia

- **Authors:** Stanghellini G, Cuffaro G
- **Year:** 2026
- **Journal:** Psychopathology (Karger)
- **DOI:** 10.1159/psp/aelag012
- **Google Scholar URL:** https://www.karger.com (exact article URL not resolved; PMID 42853770)

**Abstract:**

Phenomenology has long helped explain schizophrenia as a whole, but its application to borderline personality disorder (BPD) is fragmented; existing work on BPD tends to focus on disrupted temporality, dysphoric mood, emotion dysregulation, and bodily/interpersonal disturbances, often overlooking how strongly a reality that exists independently of consciousness can affect a person emotionally and sensorially. The authors use "phenomenological realism" to treat BPD as an integrated whole via the concept of "ontic gravity." In BPD, especially during acute distress, consciousness is marked by an "excess of reality." Comparing BPD with schizophrenia reveals a key difference in derealization: in schizophrenia reality seems to evaporate and the person searches for hidden meaning, whereas in BPD reality feels traumatic, blunt, and intrusive. Using the phenomenological epoché, the authors argue schizophrenia resembles an involuntary suspension of the natural attitude, whereas BPD reflects complete immersion in it — people with BPD cannot step back from what is immediately presented as fact. When severely distressed, people with BPD may feel "crushed" by events, taking a "direct gaze" rather than the "oblique gaze" associated with schizophrenia; their bodily feelings, encounters with others, and perceptions feel raw and physically abrasive, producing suffering rather than prompting reflection.

### 5. Anxiety Sensitivity, Psychological Vulnerability, and Brain Volumes in Borderline Personality Disorder

- **Authors:** Kılıçaslan AK, Uğur K, Sun T, Taşçı İ, Maçin G, Yıldız S
- **Year:** 2026
- **Journal:** Psychiatry Research: Neuroimaging (Elsevier)
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.pscychresns.2026.112351
- **Google Scholar URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com (exact article URL not resolved; PMID 42858654)

**Abstract:**

Aim: compare anxiety sensitivity (AS), psychological vulnerability, and brain volumes between people with BPD and healthy controls, and examine links between clinical and volumetric measures within the BPD group. Method: 27 patients with BPD and 28 healthy controls completed the Anxiety Sensitivity Index-3 and the Psychological Vulnerability Scale; regional brain volumes were measured from 3D T1-weighted MRI on a 1.5-Tesla scanner. Results: before covariate adjustment, the BPD group scored higher on psychological vulnerability and on total, cognitive, and social AS; after adjustment, only social AS remained significantly higher. Within the BPD group, posterior insula volume was negatively correlated with total and social AS after controlling for confounders; psychological vulnerability and cognitive AS were not associated with regional volumes. Conclusion: social AS may be a specific dimension of BPD; the anterior insula and striatum differ at the group level, while posterior insula volume tracks AS, suggesting distinct roles for insular subregions.

**Abstract:**

The authors systematically reviewed intravenous and intranasal racemic ketamine for adults with BPD or elevated borderline features, searching MEDLINE, Embase, and CENTRAL through February 2026. ### 10. Caring for complex emotional needs: a systematic review of structured clinical management and an empirical study of specialist team working practices

- **Authors:** Jones L
- **Year:** 2026
- **Source:** Cardiff University ORCA repository (orca.cardiff.ac.uk) — doctoral thesis
- **DOI:** Not confirmed (blocked)
- **Google Scholar URL:** https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/189786/

**Abstract:**

Objective: synthesise the evidence on Structured Clinical Management (SCM), a manualised, low-intensity intervention for adults with borderline personality disorder, using Proctor et al.'s implementation outcomes framework. Method: a mixed-methods systematic review of six databases and grey literature, including only English-language community studies of adults aged 18 or older; quality was assessed with the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool. Implementation was feasible but depended on training, supervision, and organisational support; fidelity reporting was inconsistent. Conclusion: SCM shows meaningful clinical benefits and is useful as a service-level framework, but the evidence base is sparse, relies heavily on trials by SCM's developers, and mostly covers BPD-diagnosed participants; the authors call for independent, adequately powered studies.
